## Iteration 904: else and elif may start the next line

matter - and if it does, the rule "whitespace is a delimiter" is not kept: why? It is kept, at two
levels: blanks part words, a newline (or ;) ends a command - rule 1, the shell's; Forth's newline is a
blank only because its definitions end with ;. `if c { } else { }` is one command whose words are if, c,

CMDS' on the return stack), its last word a block, and NEXT-ELSE? finding else or elif first on a later

now saying so. else-line.cm rewritten to show it working (failing on 902's image), else-no-if.cm the

## Iteration 906: \ at a line's end

The owner's yes to 905's proposal. A \ that is a word of its own at a line's end - a blank before it,
blanks or a comment after - and the command goes on to the next line: CMDS' drops the word at the
newline (NDP back to its node) and carries on, the command's node its last word for the checks that
read one (CUR-CMD); LIST-P drops one too, though a list's newline is a blank already. As the operators
are: a \ glued to a line's last word - sh's join of foo\ and the next line's first word - is an error
when read (PIECES-P), and so is a \ word elsewhere in a line (BS-AT-END: "the text \ is '\'") or at the
text's end. Inside quotes and inside a word, \ is the character. The reference says it, its example a
line of reference.cm. backslash.cm, backslash-glued.cm and backslash-mid.cm, each failing on 902's image.
tests/cm/run 117, both ways; BASELINE cm:passed 114 to 117. Image 72,574 bytes (+384).

Two causes, both in what compiles the shell and neither in the engines.

The compiler. A design's speed is in its image: the lab's converter (tools/layout.py) specialises the
kernel's call sites, gives variables slot opcodes, folds and fuses - and never sees code compiled at run
time. The shell is compiled by the kernel's own compiler, which calls every word but its thirty
primitives: a variable a call to DOVAR, a constant a call, CELLS or 2DUP or - or +! a call to a colon word.
Counted (the knee's engine built with PROFILE=1): on the Forth benchmarks a design runs 0.17-1.47 times
relf's dispatches, on the shell's scripts 3.8-4.2 times - loop.sh 56.9 million to relf's 15.1, 10.4
million of them calls to relf's 0.97. The benchmarks are primitives in tight loops; the shell is
variables, constants, small words, locals and strings - VAR@ alone is 15 % of relf's dispatches - which
relf's compiler and instruction set were made for. Per dispatch the knee is the faster (callgrind: about
1.8 ns a dispatch and 0.16 ns an instruction; LIT64, a byte loop on every design, 35 % of a build's
instructions with variables as literals).

The JIT took almost nothing. A JIT design translates a word only if it translates every word below it,
and the return stack only in order down the page. fastest-x86-64 still interpreted 32.8 million
dispatches of loop.sh: the JIT's log (JITLOG) traced every refusal to a handful of roots - the kernel's
variables (START: NOOPs, then a DOVAR it has no stencil for), SYSCALL and the kernel's SCAN (operations
without stencils), HERE and PAD (a variable's slot), the port's MOVE, STR-EQ? and COMPARE (an R> in each
arm), on best-all-round its C CMOVE - and every word above each root, up to MAIN, stayed bytecode. So the
JIT designs ran the shell as interpreters, no faster than the portable ones.

The fix is a front end for the design's compiler, in the port (the shell's sources unchanged). lab/
compile.4's COMPILE, - interp.4's INTERPRET calls it - compiles a variable or a CREATEd word as its
address, a constant as its value; the kernel's small colon words (CELLS, -, 2DUP, +!, PICK, MIN, 0<, the
@XT and !XT of 941 ...) as their primitives, inline; a colon word of up to four tokens, none of them
immediate or the return stack's, as its tokens; and POSTPONE through it (the kernel's compiled a macro's
words with the kernel's COMPILE,: pool.4's BUFFER-BODY called START, shadow.4's frames called their run-
time words). An address literal is absolute, and an image loads elsewhere each run: every site is kept,
the save writes the list (varints of the distances), and RELOCATE, PORT-BOOT's first word, moves each.
Nothing knows a design's encoding - the DOVAR byte and where a literal's cell lies come from probes. The
locals' frames are inline (shadow.4: a save and a restore of each local where relf has its LSAVE and
LREST), and the port's string words take a cell at a time while the strings agree. lab/again.4 compiles
again the words of the files the kernel's own interpreter read (compile.4, interp.4: their MOVE, WITHIN
and ABORT"'s words were the kernel compiler's). build.sh's VARLIT chooses which variables are literals:
0 none - an interpreted design's LIT64 reads its eight bytes one at a time, which costs what the call and
DOVAR saved (equal time, 34 % larger image on the knee) -, 1 all, 2 the kernel's (a JIT design's default).

On a JIT design, more. The kernel's variables are literals too (the JIT makes a call of our variables a
literal itself, from its header; not of the kernel's, which begin with NOOPs). With the gene jitexec
(fastest-x86-64, fastest-x86-32, best-all-round), native code runs EXECUTE's word in the interpreter when
it was refused, so a call of a refused word can go through EXECUTE and leave its caller translatable:
the kernel's ALLOCATE FREE RESIZE, the primitives the JIT has no stencil for - found by probes, a word that
calls the primitive past a false flag run once at the build, its JIT header then saying native or refused
(SYSCALL everywhere, CMOVE where it is C) - and by name the I/O words and the shell's few with SP! or the
return stack out of order (RUN-CAUGHT, TEST-PARSE-INT, EXPAND-AND-RUN, EXEC-LOOP, RUN-TRAP ...). The JIT
gives up sixteen calls down, and the word at the top for good: $(( )) is 21 deep from XE-TRY, so the
front end keeps each word's depth and makes a call that would pass twelve through EXECUTE (its word then
translated from the top when it first runs; the call an inline cache's hit after). lab/jitwords.4 has
CSTRLEN, SCAN, COMPARE, STR-EQ?, HERE, PAD and the pictured numbers as the JIT takes them.

One limit of the lab's JIT shaped the rest: a THROW from interpreted code that native code called
unwinds the return stack but not the machine's - the catching code goes on in the innermost interpreter,
the native caller's continuation is lost, and a later return through an outer interpreter lands in the
wrong frame. On the bare design: `: T2 ABORT ; : T3 ['] T2 CATCH . ; : T5 ['] T3 EXECUTE 5 . ; T5`
prints `-1 ` on fastest-x86-64 and best-all-round and fastest-x86-32, `-1 5 ` on the knee (ABORT is on the
JIT's cold list, so T2 is native and THROW is reached from native code). A word that calls THROW itself
is never translated, nor is any word that calls it, so the parser and the forth command stayed
interpreted from their CATCH to their THROW; the first routing broke that (`. bad-script` and `forth ':
T ABORT ; T'` ended the shell). So the front end marks a word that may throw (THROW, ABORT, (ABORT"),
(THROW), and any word that calls one - not through EXECUTE or CATCH) and never routes a call of one; a
word that reads its caller's return address (an R> or R@ before any >R) is never routed either; a forth
command's definitions get the header of a refused word (interpreted, as before - and `: X RECURSE ;` now
overflows, where the JIT made it a jump and run-forth-errors never ended); PORT-BOOT is saved as refused
(translating it would translate MAIN and the rest before RELOCATE ran: a crash at the first run, found
when RELOCATE itself became translatable). The port's fatal errors - out of memory, the locals' save stack
- still THROW across native code, as a design's faults end the shell already. The front end and strings.4's cell
loops take a cell's size from the host now, not 8 (every design's cell is 8 bytes - fastest-x86-32's
too, an x86-64 engine - so a 4-byte one is untried; corrected at 943).

How. The compare-and-branch family grows by `> = DUP` (contiguous at 0x38-0x3A, so ?BRANCH,'s rewrite
and gen-opcodes.sh's family check take them as they are). LOOP lays (LOOP)BRANCH8 and its offset where
the branch back reaches in a byte, else (LOOP) and BRANCH, in kernel.4 and in cross.4. The pairs are a
mechanism of their own: PEEP-PAIR (kernel.4, and PEEP-PAIR-T in cross.4) rewrites LAST-OP's byte when
the plain operation being compiled completes a pair in PAIR-OPS - first, second and fused, three bytes
each - and lays nothing; NO-PEEP clears LAST-OP at every branch target, as for the folds. gen-opcodes.sh
checks each fused opcode is a pair row named as its two operations joined, and the count (a swapped
table and a missing row were each caught). INL-PLAIN? copies the pairs but DUP>R, as it copies no
return-stack operation. native-cross.4 has the new words' stubs. Format 13: the kernels cross-compiled by
945's format-12 engine, then reproduced by the format-13 one.
